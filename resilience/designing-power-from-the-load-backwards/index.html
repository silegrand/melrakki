Designing power from the load backwards

Most off-grid power is sized supply first, from a datasheet. The load comes last. Here is how to do it the other way round, and let the honest answer choose the tier.

Preparedness and resilience · 8 min read

A remote mast goes dark on a still, grey morning in February. The cameras stop. The link drops. Nothing has failed. The battery is healthy, the panels are clean, the backup has fuel. The power was simply sized for a different day, in a different month, against a load nobody measured. The equipment was chosen first, and the site was left to fit around it.

That is the wrong way round, and it is common. Most off-grid power is bought supply first: a panel picked off a datasheet, a generator sized by rule of thumb, a battery chosen to match. The load, the thing that actually has to stay alive, comes last. It should come first. Sizing an off-grid power system properly means starting with the load and working back to the supply that guarantees it, through the worst conditions the site will see. Melrakki engineers every platform this way, from the load backwards.

Here is the method, in the order the numbers have to be taken.

Start with the load, not the panel

The load is every device that has to keep running, its real power draw, and when it actually draws it. A fixed camera pulls a steady figure. A PTZ camera pulls more when it moves. Infrared illumination draws nothing until the light fails, then draws hard all night. An LTE or 5G router draws in bursts when it transmits and idles between them. A heater draws hardest on the coldest day of the year, which is also the day the solar gives you least.

The nameplate on a device is a ceiling, not an average. Size everything to its ceiling and you pay for capacity you will never use. Size to a soft average and the platform goes dark the moment several loads coincide, which they will. What you want is the realistic worst-case daily draw: the load as it behaves on a bad day, not on a brochure.

Turn the load into a daily energy figure

Power is watts. Energy is watts across time, in watt-hours. This is the figure that sizes everything downstream, and it is simple arithmetic once the load is honest. A continuous 100 W draw is 2,400 watt-hours a day.

That reference matters, because it is the load the Melrakki platforms are quoted against. The published endurance figures are stated at 100 W for a reason: it is a fixed reference draw, and your site is measured against it. Double the load and you roughly halve the endurance. That relationship is unforgiving, and it is exactly why the load figure has to be measured rather than hoped for. A platform that holds for days at one draw can empty in hours at another, and nothing about the hardware changed. Only the load did.

Account for the system's own losses

Nameplate energy is not usable energy. Conversion, wiring, standby draw and the depth to which you are prepared to discharge a battery all take a share before a single watt reaches the payload.

The Harka Solar shows what this looks like in practice. It carries a 24 VDC 400 Ah pack, which is 9.6 kWh on the nameplate. Its published reserve is 75 hours at 100 W, which is 7.5 kWh of real work delivered to the load. The gap between the two figures is the loss and the discharge margin the design already respects, so that the reserve you are quoted is the reserve you actually get. Plan against nameplate and you will come up short in the field. Plan against usable energy and the numbers hold.

Set the endurance you need with no input at all

Autonomy is the question of how long the platform must hold with no generation whatsoever: a run of overcast days, an array under snow, a week of low sun. This is the reserve, and it is a buffer against the weather rather than a headline.

How large it needs to be is a site question, not a product one. A site you can reach in an afternoon needs a smaller reserve than one reached by a weekly convoy or a seasonal window. The Harka Solar holds 75 hours at 100 W from the battery alone, and runs indefinitely where irradiance supports the duty cycle. Those are two different promises. The first is what happens when the sun stops. The second is what happens when it comes back. You need to know which one your site is relying on, and in which month.

The sum is straightforward. Take the usable energy in the pack and divide by the daily draw, and you have the reserve in days. On the Harka Solar the usable figure is the 7.5 kWh that sits behind the 75 hours at 100 W. A lighter payload stretches that reserve. A heavier one shortens it. The platform does not change, only the arithmetic against your load, which is the whole reason the load has to be measured before anything else is decided.

Match generation to the worst month, not the average

This is where most off-grid solar sizing quietly fails. Solar output is not a number, it is a season. The energy a panel returns depends on peak sun hours, and those collapse at high latitude in winter, under persistent cloud, at a low sun angle, under soiling, and under snow that sits on the glass and sheds nothing. A platform sized against a summer average will run beautifully all summer and fall over in December.

Peak sun hours are not guesswork. They come from irradiance data for the exact location, broken down by month, from public meteorological and solar resource datasets. Pull the worst month for the site, not the annual mean, and size to that figure.

So you size the array against the worst month the site has to work through, then derate for the conditions that steal from it: heat, which costs a panel output; cold, which costs a battery capacity; tilt and angle; dust and dirt; and the hours the sun is simply too low to do useful work. Note the timing, because it is unkind. Cold reduces the battery's usable capacity in the same weeks that low sun reduces what you can put back into it. The worst month attacks both sides of the ledger at once, which is why an average will always flatter a site and the worst case never will. If the worst month still closes the gap between what you generate and what the load consumes, solar alone will hold the site all year. If it does not, no amount of optimism will change the physics.

Decide the tier

The tier is an output of the method, not a preference. You do not choose solar because it is clean, or hybrid because it feels safe. You measure the load and the site, and the honest answer falls out.

If generation covers the load through the hardest season, the clean tier is correct: no fuel, no emissions, no noise, the lowest signature and the lowest running cost. The Harka Solar is that answer, solar and battery alone, running indefinitely where irradiance supports the duty cycle.

If a latitude, a season or a heavy load means solar cannot close the gap on its own, there are two honest routes. You can oversize the array and the battery until they can, which works, but buys the worst week of the year with footprint, weight, cost and a lot of capacity that sits idle for the other fifty-one weeks. Or you carry fuelled backup that runs only when it is needed. The Harka Hybrid takes the second route: solar and battery carry the payload in silence, and a Stage V generator on HVO renewable diesel engages at 20% depth of discharge to recharge the pack, then shuts down. It is silent and zero emission in battery mode, and it burns fuel only in the narrow window when the sun cannot keep up. That is the endurance tier: guaranteed uptime bought with the smallest amount of fuel, on the days that genuinely need it, and none of the days that do not.

Neither tier is better. One suits a site where irradiance supports the duty cycle year round. The other exists precisely because some sites, some seasons and some loads do not allow that. The method tells you which site you have.

The numbers to bring to the meeting

Before you speak to any supplier, fill these in. With them, sizing is arithmetic. Without them, it is a guess dressed as a specification.

  • Continuous load and peak load, in watts
  • The duty cycle of each major device: what draws, and when
  • Daily energy consumed, in watt-hours
  • Required autonomy with no input, in hours or days
  • Site latitude and the worst-month peak sun hours
  • Temperature range, for battery and panel derating
  • Shading, soiling and snow exposure
  • Access interval: how often a person can physically reach the site
  • Security exposure: whether fuel is a theft or a signature problem here

Nine numbers. They decide the array, the battery, the reserve and the tier, in that order, and they decide them before anything is bought.

Where the grid ends

The mast that went dark in February did not need a bigger panel. It needed a design that started with the load and the site, not a datasheet. Every platform that holds through a bad winter was sized from the load backwards, against the worst month, with the losses counted honestly.
