Fuel independence

The true cost of a diesel generator on a remote site

The real cost of remote power is rarely the fuel. It is the convoy that delivers it, the emissions it has to account for, the signature it broadcasts and the theft it invites, and everything else required to keep a machine running where logistics are hard.

Preparedness and resilience · 12 min read

A diesel generator on a remote site looks simple on a spreadsheet. A generator, a fuel line, a service interval, electricity out. Four things you can price. The trouble is that the generator is rarely the expensive part. The expensive part is everything that has to happen around it to keep it running, in a place chosen precisely because it is hard to reach.

That is the case this note makes. Not that diesel is bad, because it is not, but that the conventional calculation understates what remote diesel generation actually costs, because most of the cost never appears as a fuel line. And once the whole cost is visible, a second question follows on its own: what changes when the power system needs less done to it?

The generator is the cheap part

On paper, a remote generator turns fuel into electricity. Generator, fuel, maintenance, output. In the field it is the final stage of a supply chain that reaches back to a refinery. Someone has to buy the fuel, store it, move it and get it onto the site down a road that is not always open. Someone has to service the machine, carry the parts, and be standing next to it when it fails. The electricity is real. So is the chain behind it, and the chain is where the money goes.

The chain runs roughly like this: a fuel supplier, a depot, a tanker, a driver, an access route, a storage tank on site, the generator itself, then servicing, spare parts and a site visit, and only at the very end, electricity. The current at the load is the last output of a surprisingly long logistics operation. Price only the first and last links and the system looks cheap. Price the whole chain and it does not.

What "remote" really means

Remote is not a distance. It is a measure of how hard, slow, risky or expensive it is to reach a site and do something useful there. A monitoring cabinet three miles off a motorway can be operationally remote if reaching it means a permit, a lane closure and a two-person team. A mast on a hillside is remote because the last mile has no road at all. The Arctic is remote, but so is any site where a routine visit is a half-day and a failure is a scramble.

Melrakki treats remoteness as a logistics problem, not a map reference, because that is how it behaves on a budget. The useful measure is not how far the site is. It is how much it costs, and how long it takes, to put a person or a litre of fuel next to the equipment.

The remote generator cost stack

It helps to see the cost as a stack rather than a single figure. This is a decision-making tool, not an accounting standard, and not every layer can be priced to the penny. But every layer is real, and most of them are missing from the fuel quote.

  • Fuel — litres burned against the load, at the delivered price, not the forecourt price
  • Fuel logistics — transport, vehicles, drivers, minimum deliveries, access, weather disruption
  • People — the technicians, engineers and security staff whose time the site consumes
  • Maintenance — oil, filters, coolant, belts, batteries, parts, and the travel to fit them
  • Downtime risk — the cost of everything that stops when the generator stops
  • Security — protecting a tank of fuel and a machine that is worth stealing
  • Emissions — carbon, air quality and the reporting that now follows both
  • Capital and end of life — buying the machine, and dealing with it when it is spent

Add those honestly and the delivered cost of remote diesel power looks very different from the number on the fuel invoice.

A litre delivered is not a litre bought

Fuel is the visible cost, and even it is usually understated. A litre at a forecourt and a litre in a tank on a remote site are not the same commodity. The second one carries the cost of getting it there: the tanker, the driver, the miles, the minimum load you have to take whether you need it or not, and the days a delivery slips because the route closed. On difficult sites, fuel that has to travel by boat, by air or up a long single-track approach can cost several times its pump price by the time it reaches the tank.

The rules moved against remote diesel as well. Since April 2022 the UK has withdrawn the rebate on red diesel for most commercial and construction use, so operators who once ran generators on rebated fuel now pay full duty, close to fifty pence a litre more, or switch to a renewable diesel such as HVO. For a machine that runs for months, that is not a rounding error. It is a structural increase in the running cost of the least efficient way to power a remote site.

The convoy problem

Every remote generator creates a recurring journey. The fuel runs down, so a vehicle goes out. The service falls due, so a technician goes out. Something fails, so someone goes out again. The generator does not only consume fuel, it consumes trips, and trips consume vehicles, roads, time and people on a schedule the site cannot pause.

That reframes the question worth asking. Not how many litres a year does the generator burn, but how many journeys a year does the power system require. A site that needs a vehicle down a bad road every fortnight carries a very different cost, and a very different risk, from one that needs a visit twice a year. The litres are visible. The journeys are where the exposure lives.

The cost of a site visit

Which makes the site visit a unit of cost worth naming in its own right. A visit is never just fuel and a wage. It is the vehicle and its depreciation, the insurance, the planning, the travel time, sometimes a ferry, a flight or a night's accommodation, the safety procedures, the second person because you cannot send anyone out alone, and the work not being done elsewhere while it happens.

The more remote the site, the more every human intervention costs, and the more a system that needs fewer of them is worth.

Autonomy is not an abstract virtue here. It is the number of visits you do not have to make, multiplied by what each one would have cost. As remoteness rises, that number climbs faster than the fuel bill ever will.

The energy signature

A running generator does more than make electricity. It announces itself. It has an acoustic signature you can hear across a valley, a thermal signature a camera can pick out in the dark, and a visual footprint, the exhaust, the tank, the fuel store, that makes a site more obvious than it needs to be. The deliveries add a logistic signature, a pattern of vehicles arriving on a rhythm. The visits add a human one.

None of that matters on every site. On some it matters a great deal. For security, surveillance and border infrastructure, a power source that is silent, cool and needs no resupply is not a green talking point, it is cover. A site that makes no noise, throws no heat and receives no convoy is simply harder to find and harder to read. That is why the clean tier exists, and why low signature sits alongside low running cost as a reason to take the generator off a site where the conditions allow it.

Fuel is worth stealing

Stored fuel is an asset, and assets on quiet sites attract attention. Diesel can be siphoned, drained, contaminated or simply carried off, and every litre stored is a litre someone else might take. Protecting it, with tanks, locks, alarms or yet more visits, is a cost the fuel quote never mentions, and one that grows on exactly the isolated sites where diesel is hardest to justify in the first place. Remove the tank and you remove the target.

Emissions are not only carbon

Emissions are part of the real cost now, and they are more than a single number. Burning a litre of diesel releases roughly two and a half to two and three-quarter kilograms of carbon dioxide on the UK government's own conversion factors, before the emissions of getting that litre to site are counted at all. Beyond carbon there is NOx and particulate matter, local air quality and noise, each of which can matter near people, near habitats or under the terms of a permit.

There are two layers to account for: what leaves the exhaust on site, and what it took to deliver the fuel there in the first place. Generating the energy locally removes the second layer entirely, because there is no delivery to make.

What happens when the generator stops

The largest cost is the one that only appears on a bad day. When a remote generator stops, the site does not lose a machine, it loses everything the machine was keeping alive. Cameras go dark. Radios and networks drop. Telemetry, alarms, access control and environmental monitoring stop at once, and on some sites data is lost that cannot be recovered.

So the cost of the failure is rarely the cost of the repair. It is the cost of the hours the site was blind, unheard and unprotected while someone drove out to it. A power system should be judged on what it protects, not only on what it costs to fix.

What autonomous power changes

Autonomous power is a system designed to generate, store, manage and deliver electricity on site, with as little routine human intervention and external supply as the site allows. Not a panel on a pole. A designed system: generation, storage, power electronics, monitoring and a maintenance strategy, sized to a real load.

What it removes is the logistics. Done properly, an autonomous platform can take away the routine fuel deliveries, the fuel store and its theft risk, the exposure to fuel price and duty, the noise and heat, and a large share of the servicing visits and the journeys that carried them. On the sites Melrakki builds for, that is the difference between a power source that needs tending and one that mostly looks after itself.

It is not maintenance free

It is important not to oversell that. Autonomous power does not abolish dependency, it substitutes one set for another. A diesel system depends on fuel, logistics and servicing. A solar and battery system depends on the solar resource, the health of the battery, the quality of the design and the conditions on site. Both need monitoring. Both need occasional hands.

The honest claim is not zero intervention. It is fewer interventions, and fewer things that have to be carried to the site to keep it running. That is a smaller promise than the marketing usually makes, and a more durable one.

The last mile of energy

There is a useful way to hold all of this in a single idea. The energy is rarely the expensive part. Getting it to the site, again and again, is. A litre of diesel is cheap where it is refined and dear where it is burned, and almost all of the difference is delivery.

Autonomy attacks that last mile directly. It does not try to make the energy cheaper. It removes the need to keep carrying it in. That is where its economic value comes from: not a lower fuel price, but the deletion of the logistics wrapped around the fuel.

A better comparison than "solar versus diesel"

None of this means autonomous power always wins. It means the usual comparison is the wrong one. The question is not which fuel is cheaper per litre or per kilowatt-hour. It is which system requires the least to be done to it to keep the site operational, across the whole time it has to run.

Answering that needs the site, not a slogan: the load and its duty cycle, the solar resource in the worst month rather than the average, the storage the reserve demands, the length of the deployment, how hard the site is to reach, how much a failure costs, and redundancy, capital and access weighed together. We set out how to run that calculation in sizing a system from the load backwards, because the honest answer, clean, hybrid or conventional, falls out of the numbers rather than the preference.

When diesel is still the right answer

Diesel earns its place in plenty of cases. It has high energy density, predictable output and rapid deployment, it runs for long durations on demand, and it is backed by a mature maintenance ecosystem. For a short project on an accessible site, for a very heavy or unpredictable load, for a genuine emergency where a machine has to be running within the hour, a generator is often the correct tool. The argument here is not that generators are obsolete. It is that the sites where diesel quietly costs the most are exactly the ones where its true cost is least visible.

When autonomy starts to pay

The crossover is a site question, and it moves in one direction. The harder a site is to reach, the longer it has to run, and the more its signature and its continuity matter, the more the logistics around a generator cost, and the more an autonomous platform is worth. On an easy site for a few weeks, diesel is hard to beat. On a remote site for a year, where every visit is expensive and every failure is a scramble, the machine that needs the least brought to it usually wins, and often by a wide margin.

Between those poles sits the hybrid case: clean power carrying the load in silence, with fuelled backup that runs only in the narrow window the sun cannot cover. That is the Harka Hybrid's job. It stays silent and zero emission on battery, and burns fuel only on the days that genuinely need it, which is a very different fuel bill, and a very different signature, from a generator that runs around the clock.

Where the grid ends

A remote generator does not only consume diesel. It consumes logistics, time, people, access, security, maintenance and attention, and it goes on consuming them for as long as the site runs. Almost none of that reaches the fuel line, which is why the cheap-looking option is so often the expensive one.

Autonomous power changes the equation by asking a different question. Not how do we fuel this site, but what if the energy system could take care of more of itself. That is the whole idea behind an autonomous platform, and it is why Melrakki designs power around a site's dependencies rather than around a machine.
